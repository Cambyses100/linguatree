Moon 3 Day 11 - Make the Point for the Joint

Keep the working spear intact

The original single-point wooden spear, used for yesterday’s fishing, stays beside the entrance. The Multi-Pronged Fishing Spear is still unmade. For the next hunting tool you want a separate shaft and a replaceable stone point. A sharper tip may be useful, but its edge cannot compensate for a weak seat or a splitting shaft. Today you will prepare compatible parts; assembly and a controlled load test can follow when the fit is ready.

On the accessible woodland edge you inspect fallen oak growth. One straight section has remained off wet ground, with sound wood beneath the weathered surface. You reject an attractive piece whose split follows a knot into the intended head. The selected length is about your height, with enough diameter at its thicker end to leave wood on both sides of a future seat.

You remove the remaining bark and small branch projections. The shaft is reasonably straight as selected; there is no instant fire-straightening or claim that a dry surface proves the centre seasoned. It rests under cover while you make the insert.

Choose stone for the mounting as well as the edge

A fresh visit to the known gravel source supplies a sound small blank, separate from the useful cutting edges you already carry. At a cleared knapping place outside the living floor, you use the familiar Hammerstone and Bifacial Knapping. Sharp waste stays away from the entrance, food and open toes.

The intended Stone Spear Point is a short stout leaf form, roughly a finger-length long and two finger-widths across. You retain thickness along the middle. Controlled alternate removals shape a working tip and leave a broad base that can sit in wood. This is not an attempt to reproduce a long thin ceremonial blade or a point copied exactly from a distant excavation.

You stop thinning when the blank already suits the job. The base’s sharp projections are reduced on a coarse expendable stone so they will not cut the binding. The forward working edges remain sharp. Minor asymmetry matters less than a sound body and a base that can be supported.

Compare the two parts before committing

With the shaft lying on the craft stone, you hold the point alongside its end and compare width, thickness and remaining wood. The stone lies separately on bark when you put it down; it is not lodged into an unbound split for display. There is enough shaft to make the intended shallow seat, but its exact fit is tomorrow’s work.

You wrap the point for storage away from food and feet. New line will be made for the binding rather than silently borrowed from the cap, trap or screen. Pitch may eventually help a joint, but glue is not being used to hide an unsupported gap.

The local people already use composite spears. Your contribution to this handbook is to explain the load path and its dependencies clearly, not to claim you have invented their weapon. A completed point, an assembled spear and a successful hunting system are three different states.

What has been drying meanwhile

The wall trial has now had several mild drying intervals. One witness patch breaks with a firm centre rather than smearing. Fine shrinkage lines show near an exposed edge; the installed small bay remains keyed to its rods under a light hand check, without a falling flake or shifted support. You leave its surface visible for inspection and retain the bark rain protection. This justifies keeping the trial, not coating the entire front or promising freeze resistance.

You finish with a usable new point and a selected shaft, still separate. Next comes the joint: shape the seat around the actual stone, restrain the wood, bind it, and test small loads before trusting a thrust. The warmer working home is beginning to make that sustained technical sequence possible.
