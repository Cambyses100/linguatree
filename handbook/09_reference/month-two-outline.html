Month Two Outline

The author approved this outline on 3 October 2026. It is a planning framework, not an extension of the event ledger. Month 2 means the next four narrative weeks, Days 29–56, following the first twenty-eight-day block. Daily entries, acquisition dates and new capability pages will be built from this approved framework after establishing the area map. Dates below identify broad phases; they do not force a successful experiment on a particular day.

Where we want to be on Day 56

The traveller is still alone, but his camp, clothing and working routines have begun to support a colder season. He has made the atlatl into a useful practice system through his own work. It has not made him a skilled hunter or erased the advantage of experienced local spear users. The neighbours have noticed his improvement and sometimes choose to watch. They have not joined him, supplied him or agreed to protect him.

The month’s main question is: can one person turn a precarious camp into a more resilient way of living before cold and diminishing harvest access outrun his labour? The answer is a qualified improvement. Winter survival remains the next pressure; he has not accumulated enough food or clothing to declare that problem solved.

The starting constraints

Read Survival Ledger before drafting each day; First Month Ledger preserves the opening inventory. The Day 28 state includes:

  • 2.59 kg of edible hazelnut kernels, roughly five days at the recent half-kilogram daily use. This excludes shells and does not represent a month of food.
  • 16 kg of usable dry fuel, roughly three days at recent ordinary consumption, before extra firing or colder-weather demand.
  • 2.6 m of loose nettle cord and 0.8 m of bark cord, plus 16 m of uneven single yarn. Yarn is not a substitute for tested load-bearing line.
  • 44 g of prepared nettle fibre, a replacement wooden spindle and 0.7 m of reserved sinew thread. Significant new clothing, bindings and traps need further collection and processing.
  • One experimental fish cage, three deadfall trials, a damaged snare already removed from service, a repaired bark vessel and a leaking pot retained only as a specimen.
  • An assembled but untested atlatl, two green shaft candidates and a small feather lot. There are no finished darts.
  • Very limited clothing: bark panels, a small pelvic pad and the narrow fox shoulder panel. The first-month fox skin has already been allocated; it cannot become another garment.

The second month cannot repeat first-month hazel yields indefinitely. Falling availability, weather and territorial boundaries should begin to affect the gathering circuit. Cooler weather can also slow drying and fibre processing. A material’s elapsed calendar time is never its acceptance test.

Four phases

Days 29–35 — Make room for the next month

The first priority is continued food, water, fuel and warmth. Atlatl work proceeds alongside those needs, rather than filling the whole day.

Technical work: inspect the shaft candidates, make an expendable practice projectile only when its condition permits, and establish a clear range with an earth backstop away from occupied routes. Short controlled attempts reveal release or fit problems. Introduce a marked wooden reference staff and simple comparison records. Start a further independently collected fibre batch early, while using current fibre carefully. Repair the windward shelter cover and make a small mat or insulating bed layer before attempting a large garment.

Defence: identify an alternative site and route before needing them. Protect spare tinder and usable fuel. Keep the spear reachable during vulnerable work and ensure clothing and essential implements can be taken quickly. Spread brush for a windbreak or to make one approach more visible, not as an impregnable wall. Maintain the separation of food, dirty processing, waste and bedding.

Narrative movement: he sees local people overlooking his part of the valley or passing within sight of his camp. They do not attack, but their attention removes any comforting assumption that he is hidden. He decides what he would abandon to get away. This does not create a theft or injury that the ledger must silently absorb.

End of phase: preliminary casts and small practical camp improvements; new fibre and ceramic trials started but unfinished. A clothing problem is already being worked on, rather than postponed until a hunt supplies a hide.

Days 36–42 — Curiosity without admiration

Technical work: continued short practice sessions; diagnose shaft straightness, engagement, release and attachments one change at a time. Improve the existing fish cage’s closure, weave gaps, anchoring or location after inspection, with catch and empty checks recorded. Test a revised small-game mechanism on non-animal loads before putting scarce cord back into service. Process newly acquired pelts while they are useful; stitch patches and trial softening/conditioning on small pieces. Prepare small ceramic comparison samples and let drying proceed while other work continues.

The first observed atlatl use: a few neighbours notice practice on open ground, outside both camps. One watches the lever and the dart’s flight with clear interest. The curiosity is about its operation; it does not mean they have been impressed by the result. A strong, practised adult makes a few hand-thrown casts with his own spear. His throws are visibly more forceful and much more accurate than the traveller’s unstable attempts. The traveller loses the comparison despite the device’s potential.

Keep this encounter brief and wary. People recover their own equipment; nobody lends a spear, gives a lesson or supplies a point. Individual robust physique, long practice, good equipment and the traveller’s poor form explain this result. It is not a species-wide law about strength. A different local person may warn him away from a route or gathering patch later in the week. Curious observers do not speak for the whole group.

End of phase: the traveller has learned what his proposed advantage actually needs. He returns to material and technique work, without being accepted as a teacher. Clothing and camp repairs continue to matter more than proving himself to an audience.

Days 43–49 — The hunting problem is larger than the weapon

Hunting work: one scouting outing maps tracks, wind, approaches and safe exits. A later approach to red deer, already present in the setting, fails when terrain or an alert animal exposes him. Another attempt may end without a cast because the shot or retrieval would be poor. He can experiment with his ordinary spear and later a tested dart; he does not have companions to move animals, cover another exit, help with recovery or defend him during processing.

The narrative should show why a stronger throw would not solve those problems. A promising opportunity can consume half a day and leave him hungry with no new hide. Failed outings must reduce work elsewhere or draw on a finite reserve. Do not provide several simultaneous workshops while he is away.

Technical work: use the failures to decide which improvement is worth making. Continue target practice, shaft repair and tracking observations. If sound material, adequate binding and joint trials permit, a separately made stone-pointed spear becomes an optional upgrade; the knife’s light-work hafting success does not certify it. Fishing and small-game capture remain more accessible supplements than ambitious deer hunts.

The first worthwhile small ceramic firing occurs only when samples are dry and extra fuel has been collected. A wet spell or repeated ground frost exposes gaps in bedding, covering and drying arrangements. Some craft work is delayed so he can maintain warmth and ordinary supply.

Defence incident: an animal investigates the dirty-material flank or food-handling area. He notices in time and withdraws or guards his distance; the outcome is neither a heroic predator kill nor proof that fire deters everything. Improve waste handling and the warning arrangement afterwards. A serious bite or spear wound is not needed to communicate the risk.

End of phase: hunting is now an observed labour and coordination problem. The first substantial core covering is in use, and experimental items have either passed limited checks or been kept out of service.

Days 50–56 — Progress that someone else can see

Technical work: repeat the useful settings instead of starting another invention for its own sake. Improve dart consistency, release and grouping. Maintain the fish cage and the small number of active traps he can inspect. Test the ceramic survivor through repeated water holding; retain the bark treatment route. Repair clothing after actual walking, damp weather and work. Evaluate the food and fuel buffers against the colder routine.

Hunting: a modest opportunistic success is possible if the preceding entries earn it, but the recommended outline does not require a deer kill. Small catches can supply useful meat and pelt patches without ending the food problem. Missing the right opportunity remains a valid outcome, even as target performance improves.

Renewed interest: a familiar observer returns or deliberately waits near the practice place. The traveller’s casts are now recognisably steadier and more useful. At a distance he can repeatedly control, the gap has narrowed enough to sustain attention. The strongest local thrower remains better overall. A longer look, a gesture towards the hook or a cautious request to see another cast provides the ending; no one joins his camp or becomes an apprentice.

End-of-month decision: he must choose how much of the remaining autumn to allocate to food and clothing, how much to projectile practice, and whether staying at this exposed camp is still wise. Interest is a possibility for later contact, not protection against a change of mood.

Clothing without a convenient large hide

Start with a plant-based solution to wind and exposed core, while captures supply additions. A coarse wrap or mantle is a different proposition from spinning and weaving metres of fine cloth. Matting, stuffing and replacement of dry insulation can use much coarser locally tested material. Wet or compacted stuffing may perform poorly; mobility, drying and chafing have to be observed.

Independently caught rabbits and other already established small quarry supply individual skins, meat and occasional sewing inputs. Each skin is small. Several may improve chest, back, head or foot protection; they cannot simultaneously furnish a coat, bag, boots and bedding. Aim for useful core coverage assembled from mixed materials. Provide sufficient plant-based covering even if pelt returns disappoint, rather than making survival depend on a fixed number of captures.

Make early foot protection a modest field trial. Wrapped coverings or a coarse sole with replaceable insulation can relieve cold and rough-ground exposure but can also slip, stay wet or abrade. They do not instantly become fitted waterproof boots. A head covering and better bedding may offer more benefit per unit of scarce material than another decorative fur strip.

The recommended ending uses no required large-mammal pelt. A successful self-hunt would open another branch, but its hide still needs processing and its meat creates urgent preservation or disposal work. It must not become a solved winter outfit the same afternoon.

Defence as a continuing constraint

The local people have generally warned or watched, not attempted to kill him. He has no grounds to assume their intentions are permanently benign, and no realistic ability to defeat several skilled adults. Their knowledge of his camp’s area is itself consequential.

Defence therefore concerns noticing, avoiding, leaving and surviving exposure after leaving. Shelter improvements should allow him to leave quickly. Keep a ready bundle of essentials instead of imagining he can carry the whole workshop. The alternative site needs its own surveyed water access, shelter work and a small dry provision; moving supplies there reduces what remains at the main camp. An escape cache is not a second full inventory.

A simple non-injuring noise warning is worth a small trial if it is made without consuming necessary trap or shelter cord. False alarms, weather and exhaustion limit it. No injurious perimeter traps, underground gauntlet or palisade appears as a substitute for people. Fire stays outside the debris shelter; a larger blaze can consume the fuel reserve and advertise his location.

Allow the fear to alter decisions: shorten a practice session when watched, leave a contested patch, inspect an approach before concentrating on stitching, or keep a valuable object at camp rather than taking it to another uncertain meeting. Show costs and restraint, not an attack every few days.

Rules for the atlatl comparison and contact

The atlatl launches a matched dart, not an arbitrary heavy spear. Local adults throw their own spears by hand. Compare the two users and complete weapon systems honestly; do not turn unlike heads, masses and target conditions into a numerical energy test. The early local casts should visibly outperform his in practical reach, control and useful impact. Later progress is clearest in repeated grouping and stable flight rather than a single lucky longest throw.

Different adults can have different responses. Larger robust build and stronger practiced movements may contribute to the selected local expert’s advantage, along with decades of experience and better prepared equipment. Do not claim that every Neanderthal is larger or stronger than every sapiens. The traveller may improve equipment faster than bodily technique. Hunger, cold, clothing interference and arm fatigue can also affect sessions.

The first curious encounter arises from people seeing practice; he does not immediately make another persuasive visit to their camp. He keeps the flight path away from them, lowers the equipment before anyone approaches and ends the session if the distance becomes uncomfortable. We can show curiosity in actions without translating a precise sentence or inferring agreement.

All materials, tools, food, clothing and improvements come from his own work. No trading, gifts, loans, communal meals, collected cast-off equipment, scavenged group kills or recovery of the abandoned spindle enters this month. He can see what others do and recognise their competence, but no instruction or labour from them subsidises his output. Their spear demonstrations use and retain their own equipment. Acknowledging their skill and limited gestures do not become shared production.

Labour, seasonal pressure and branches

Protect ordinary supply first. A typical day might require 4–6 hours for food, water, fuel, checks and camp work, leaving 1–3 hours for one principal project and a brief practice session when conditions permit. These are draft planning allowances; later daily events must show real collection, processing, travel and repair costs. Rest and sleep remain necessary. Long hunts, firings and bad-weather repairs replace craft work, not accumulate on top of it.

Use repeated routines in the ledger without retelling identical scenes. Select different narrative problems: a rope join, a cold morning, a contested route, a failed approach to prey, a promising clay specimen or a watcher waiting for another cast. A successful incremental improvement can stand without an obligatory failure immediately beforehand.

Pages to add when the days establish them

Existing pages such as Atlatl Dart, Projectile Testing, Measurement and Records, Simple Cordage, Acorn Leaching, Pottery and Fur-On Pelt Clothing can develop as demonstrated work warrants it. Their current metadata stays unchanged until an authored event establishes the relevant milestone.

Candidate new subjects include dry insulating plant material, prepared dressing stock, a marked measuring staff, wooden wedges and mallet, a coarse mat or mantle, foot coverings, a non-injuring warning device and a distinct pelt-conditioning process. These are prospective titles, not newly acquired capabilities. Apply Category Specification to the actual subject: stocks are materials, practiced control is a skill, the object is equipment, and a distinct production route or continuing arrangement is a process/system. Extend existing pages when that avoids duplicating the same method.

Evidence informing the proposal

R17 and R21 in Research Register support taking hand-thrown weapons and local expertise seriously; neither predicts a particular fictional adult’s result. John Whittaker’s coaching account explains why release, engagement and technique require practice. It informs the learning problems, not a guaranteed one-month performance curve.

Harrison’s experimental pottery firing report supports examining local bodies, preparation and firing as an experimental sequence; it is not evidence for this valley’s clay or fuel yield. Smithsonian Museum Conservation Institute’s bibliographic record on buckskin preparation identifies conditioning and working as distinct from simply drying a hide. It does not establish a fur-on recipe for these small animals or transfer a whole tanning operation into an afternoon.

The exact new methods, local plant suitability, hunting performance and storage losses need targeted evidence checks when the daily work is drafted. The outline establishes desired questions and modest outcomes, not validated field instructions.
