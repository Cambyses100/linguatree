Research Register

How to use this register

Sources support specific claims, not entire pages. Notes list identifiers in sources and explain their scope in the body. All links below were checked during the October 2026 editorial review. Abstract-level access is identified where full text was unavailable. The author summaries are paraphrases.

Historical evidence elsewhere does not establish presence in the fictional valley. Modern safety guidance identifies constraints; it does not certify an improvised prehistoric process. Uncited manufacturing specifications remain physical-reasoning drafts requiring experimental review.

R01 — Neanderthal cordage

Hardy et al. (2020), Direct evidence of Neanderthal fibre technology and its cognitive and behavioral implications, Scientific Reports. Direct evidence includes a three-ply inner-bark cord fragment from Abri du Maras. This supports fibre-working ability, not a known spindle inventory for every group. Abstract/search access; publication identity also checked through PubMed.

R02 — Regional coexistence

Djakovic, Key and Soressi (2022), Optimal linear estimation models predict 1400–2900 years of overlap between Homo sapiens and Neandertals prior to their disappearance from France and northern Spain. Full article inspected. The overlap is modelled at regional scale; direct local interaction remains unresolved. Do not translate it into a dated occupation of this fictional valley.

R03 — Manufactured adhesives

Identifying Palaeolithic birch tar production techniques: challenges from an experimental biomolecular approach (2023), Scientific Reports. Supports ancient manufactured adhesives and the difficulty of distinguishing production methods. It does not establish the traveller’s pine-resin recipe or its strength.

R04 — Hard-hammer handaxes

Taphonomic and technological analyses of Lower Palaeolithic bone tools from Clacton-on-Sea, UK (2022). Includes discussion of rough handaxes manufactured with hard-hammer removals and later refined work with organic percussors. Supports making antler an improvement or method option, rather than an absolute gate for every biface.

R05 — Fire treatment and wood strength

“Fire hardening” spear wood does slightly harden it, but makes it much weaker and more brittle (2016). Abstract inspected. The experiment found increased hardness alongside reduced strength and fracture performance. Effects depend on treatment and wood; do not represent charring as a universal durability upgrade.

R06 — Drinking water treatment

CDC: About Water Treatment Options When Hiking, Camping, or Traveling. Full page inspected. Clear water can carry pathogens; observable boiling is a practical endpoint. Boiling does not remove chemical contamination. Temperature-time claims need an actual means of verification.

R07 — Meat drying and pathogen control

USDA FSIS: Jerky and Food Safety. Guidance inspected. Low-temperature drying can leave pathogens alive. Its modern validated procedures use controlled heat and measurements absent from the initial camp. The revised narrative consumes game on the catch day and does not count unvalidated smoked meat as stored rations.

R08 — Plant identification

BfR: Odour test with pitfalls — what to look out for when collecting wild garlic. Supports the danger of confusing edible plants with poisonous lookalikes. Neither smell alone nor broad rules about colour, leaf count or bitterness are a reliable general identification procedure.

R09 — Giant hogweed

National Park Service: Species Spotlight — Giant Hogweed. Describes sap-associated phytophotodermatitis. This plant is removed from the setting’s friction-fire materials. Other plant candidates still need species-specific review.

R10 — Palaeolithic ceramics

Smithsonian Museum Conservation Institute record: Dolni Vestonice ceramics and Smithsonian Human Origins artifact scan. Supports Upper Palaeolithic ceramic objects at this later site. It does not support the old claim that Neanderthals made ceramic figurines, or demonstrate cooking pottery in Iberia at 40,000 BP.

R11 — Nettle extraction

Bacci et al. (2011), Effect of different extraction methods on fiber quality of nettle, Textile Research Journal. Abstract inspected. Compares several extraction routes. Cultivar, temperature and processing conditions limit transfer to wild prehistoric plants. The narrative’s twelve-day ret is an authored successful batch, not a universal duration.

R12 — Planetary defence

NASA: Planetary Defense — DART. Supports kinetic-impact deflection as one demonstrated modern technique. It does not imply that this fictional massive object can be diverted by a DART-sized mission or that the protagonist can skip space-industry dependencies.

R13 — Food composition reference

UK Government: Composition of foods integrated dataset (CoFID). Dataset landing page inspected; no specific cell was extracted for the first-month ledger. The ledger uses a declared approximate energy model rather than presenting a database-derived value. Wild plants, edible yield and activity requirements need separate research.

R14 — Neanderthal fire-making

Sorensen et al. (2018), Neandertal fire-making technology inferred from microwear analysis. Supports an interpretation of flint-and-pyrite fire-making evidence. It does not prove that every group relied on the same method or needed the traveller to supply ignition knowledge.

R15 — Cantabrian environmental mosaics

Fernández-García et al. (2023), Reassessing palaeoenvironmental conditions during the Middle to Upper Palaeolithic transition in the Cantabrian region, Quaternary Science Reviews 301, 107928. Publisher abstract and highlights inspected during the illustration pass. The synthesis describes variable conditions and open meadows with scattered forested areas during MIS 3. It supports the general landscape mosaic, not the exact fictional terrace, a reconstructed day’s weather, productive hazel patches at this date or the species of every illustrated plant. The valley’s local resource abundance remains an author assumption.

R16 — European spear-thrower evidence

Lombard (2022), Re-considering the origins of Old World spearthrower-and-dart hunting, Quaternary Science Reviews. Publisher abstract/search material inspected; full text not accessed. Direct European examples discussed include Le Placard around 25,852–25,432 and Combe Saunière 1 around 24,619–23,934 calibrated BP. These later finds do not establish the absence of earlier perishable devices, or any particular Neanderthal group’s toolkit. The narrative treats unfamiliarity at 40,000 BP as possible, not proven.

R17 — Hand-thrown wooden spears

Milks et al. (2019), External ballistics of Pleistocene hand-thrown spears: experimental performance data and implications for human evolution, Scientific Reports 9, 820; author-hosted paper. Publication abstract/search material inspected. Experienced throwers using replica Schöningen wooden spears demonstrate why such weapons should not automatically be described as short-range ineffective tools. This is an experimental comparison involving much older artefacts, not direct measurement of late Neanderthal performance. It does not predict an inexperienced traveller’s advantage.

R18 — Neanderthal anatomy for illustration

Smithsonian Human Origins: Homo neanderthalensis and How do we know these are different species?. Pages inspected. Use low foreheads, prominent brow ridges, projecting midfaces, large noses and compact robust builds with relatively short distal limbs. The intended contrast is anatomical, not a sapiens face in fur. Ordinary upright posture and capable hands preserve their humanity; individual hair, skin, clothing and expressions remain casting choices.

R19 — Wild-game meat and parasites

CDC: About Trichinellosis and CDC DPDx: Trichinellosis. Pages inspected. Foxes and other wild carnivores can carry Trichinella. Cooking and avoiding raw-to-cooked contamination matter; drying, smoking or freezing wild game are not reliable substitutes. Modern temperature verification requires a thermometer. This supports explicit uncertainty in Day 23’s immediate cooked-food decision, not an unsupported claim that the whole catch was contaminated or that visual doneness certifies safety.

R20 — Fixed overhand loop

NetKnots: Overhand Loop Knot. Instructions inspected. The terminal eye is formed by tying an overhand knot in a doubled bight of the same cord. It is a fixed loop and can jam; it is not the same as a single-strand stopper knot. Returning the long line through this eye forms a separate adjustable-loop arrangement. The source supports the fixed knot’s structure, not prehistoric attestation, natural-fibre strength or a validated spring-snare installation.

R21 — Neanderthal wooden and stone-pointed spears

Rots et al. (2021), A Leaf Point Documents Hunting with Spears in the Middle Paleolithic at Hohle Fels, Germany, Mitteilungen der Gesellschaft für Urgeschichte 30, DOI 10.51315/mgfu.2021.30004. Publisher-indexed text inspected; the full page was blocked by verification. Functional analysis supports stone weapon tips and hafting in European Middle Palaeolithic contexts. A pointed stone alone does not establish spear use.

Russo et al. (2023), First direct evidence of lion hunting and the early use of a lion pelt by Neanderthals, Scientific Reports 13, 16405. Full relevant sections inspected. The Siegsdorf lesion is interpreted as a wooden thrusting-spear wound; the discussion notes continuing wooden weapons alongside likely stone-tipped weaponry. Both choices are therefore defensible. This supports the fictional group’s composite spear, not an exact reconstruction of a northern Iberian specimen, a claim about relative prevalence at 40,000 BP, or automatic superiority in every task.

R22 — Atlatl engagement and coached practice

John Whittaker, How to throw with an atlatl, World Atlatl Association, revised 20 April 2023. Relevant coaching text inspected. It describes coordinated motion and engagement/release errors through modern observation and teaching. This supports distinguishing early disengagement, dragging and movement problems. It does not certify a particular green hazel specimen, predict a one-month learning curve or establish prehistoric regional presence.

R23 — Twining structure

Western Australian Museum: Twined Stitch and UCL Digital Egypt: Basketry techniques. Relevant text inspected. These museum/university descriptions distinguish paired crossing working elements around foundations from coiling and simple interlacing. They support the structure described in Twining, not its attestation in the fictional valley. The willow-strip, nettle-line bedding pad is an authored trial.

R24 — Palaeolithic plant bedding

Wadley et al. (2011), Middle Stone Age bedding construction and settlement patterns at Sibudu, South Africa, Science 334, 1388–1391, DOI 10.1126/science.1213317. Indexed abstract material consulted. It reports plant bedding around 77,000 years ago. This establishes plant bedding as more than a modern imagined comfort, but does not demonstrate woven mats, Iberian plant species, the traveller’s insulation performance or a transferable insect-repellent treatment. The present dry-grass/leaf choice remains a setting assumption and physical trial.

R25 — Lunar phases and reflection rhythm

NASA Science: Moon Phases. Relevant phase-cycle material inspected. A new-to-new phase cycle averages about 29.5 days; new and full moon alternate approximately halfway through it. This supports a roughly fortnightly rhythm, not a perfectly repeating fifteen-day half-cycle. The Day 0 epoch, rounded review days and sky/weather choices are fictional author bookkeeping, not a reconstructed ancient calendar. No dated 40,000 BP ephemeris is asserted.

R26 — Plant coverings and the limits of analogy

South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology: Clothing, relevant clothing and grass-mat sections inspected on 5 October 2026. The museum describes grass and other plant material in the much later Iceman equipment, and explicitly treats the former grass-cape interpretation as disputed. It does not establish a cape at the traveller’s date. The handbook therefore does not invoke an “Ötzi cape” as proof for its authored back panel.

Textile Museum of Canada: Rain Cape, collection record, search-indexed material inspected; direct retrieval failed. The record identifies grass/bark and twined/knotted construction. British Museum: palm-leaf rain cape, search-indexed object description inspected. These later ethnographic objects show that plant coverings can be wearable equipment. Their plants, structures, climate and treatment do not supply the local grass recipe, waterproof performance or prehistoric attestation. Days 39–40 remain small fictional physical trials supported structurally by R23.

R17’s hand-spear experiment and R22’s coaching account were rechecked for the Day 37 comparison. They inform the role of experience and movement, not that particular adult’s exact force, a species-wide strength rule or the traveller’s rate of improvement.

R27 — Earlier indirect spear-thrower evidence

Coppe, Taipale and Rots (2023), Terminal ballistic analysis of impact fractures reveals the use of spearthrower 31 ky ago at Maisières-Canal, Belgium, Scientific Reports 13, 18305. Abstract and relevant introduction/results inspected on 5 October 2026. Experimental fracture comparisons support the authors’ inference of spear-thrower delivery around 31,000 BP. This is trace-based evidence from stone armatures, not a preserved thrower. It supplements R16 without establishing a device in the fictional valley or proving absence at 40,000 BP.

R28 — Sewing and clothing chronology

Gilligan et al. (2024), Paleolithic eyed needles and the evolution of dress, Science Advances 10, eadp2887. Abstract and thermal-origins material inspected. The synthesis reports early eyed needles around 40,000 cal BP in Siberia, around 38,000 in the Caucasus and by about 26,000 in Europe, while discussing fitted clothing made with other sewing tools. These are preserved examples, not universal invention dates. The traveller’s Day 12 needle is a knowledgeable experiment, not established local kit. The source does not validate his blank, labour estimate or winter garments.

R29 — Very early plant bedding

Wadley et al. (2020), Fire and grass-bedding construction 200 thousand years ago at Border Cave, South Africa, Science 369, 863–866, DOI 10.1126/science.abc7239. Indexed abstract inspected. Reports grass bedding at least 200,000 years ago. This extends R24 without establishing woven mats, Iberian materials, insulating thickness or a transferable pest-control recipe.

R30 — Neanderthal specialized hide-working tools

Soressi et al. (2013), Neandertals made the first specialized bone tools in Europe, PNAS 110, 14186–14190, DOI 10.1073/pnas.1302730110. Indexed abstract and experimental-description material inspected; full-page retrieval returned verification. Rib lissoirs are interpreted as specialized hide-working tools using form, wear and replication. This supports sophisticated material work, not a complete reconstruction of clothes or a tanning recipe for the traveller.

R31 — Pleistocene woodworking and revised dating

Leder et al. (2024), The wooden artifacts from Schöningen’s Spear Horizon and their place in human evolution, PNAS, DOI 10.1073/pnas.2320484121. Indexed abstract inspected. The rich assemblage makes woodworking visible beyond a single spear. Revised age for Schöningen hunting spears indicates intensification of Neanderthal cooperative behavior around 200,000 years ago (2025), Science Advances, DOI 10.1126/sciadv.adv0752, indexed abstract inspected. This proposes a younger age than the roughly 300,000 BP estimate in older literature. Do not present the earlier estimate as settled or the revision as a universal starting date for wooden weapons.

R32 — Cold exposure and enclosed combustion

CDC: Preventing Hypothermia and CDC Yellow Book: Heat and Cold Illness in Travelers, relevant prevention and heat-loss material inspected on 5 October 2026. Wet exposure, wind, inadequate insulation and depleted energy matter even without extreme subzero weather. This supports a joint shelter/clothing/food assessment, not a calculated survival limit for the authored lean-to.

CDC: Carbon monoxide poisoning — questions and answers, indexed institutional summary inspected. Wood combustion can accumulate CO in enclosed or partially enclosed spaces. The proposed small combustible sleeping enclosure keeps its hearth outside; a smoke opening alone is not a certified exhaust design.

R33 — Acorn processing and storage analogy

National Park Service: Tools and Trade and Plant Use, relevant ethnobotanical material inspected on 5 October 2026. These institutional accounts describe stored acorns, pounding and water leaching in Californian Indigenous practice. They support treating untreated acorns, processed meal and storage as separate stages. They do not supply the Iberian oak species, local yield, a validated hot-stone/bark-vessel procedure or the traveller’s labour and edible-output estimates. The Day 42–44 small-charge results remain authored trials; reduced astringency is not a universal chemical acceptance test.

R34 — Wildlife attractants and the limits of deterrence

National Park Service: Wolf Ecology, safety and ecology material inspected on 5 October 2026. The institutional account describes wolves associating accessible food with human-use places and advises avoiding approach and unattended food remnants. It supports cleanup and distance as parts of risk management. Yellowstone animals, habituation history and visitor equipment are not a reconstruction of prehistoric Iberian wolf behaviour. The Day 47 canid visit, departure and lack of loss are authored events; speech, a spear and an outside fire are not guaranteed deterrents. Cleanup does not make the camp predator-proof.

R35 — Red-deer vigilance and cover

Jayakody, Sibbald, Gordon and Lambin (2008), Red deer Cervus elaphus vigilance behaviour differs with habitat and type of human disturbance, Wildlife Biology 14, 81–91. Abstract and study methods inspected on 5 October 2026. The field study records differing vigilance with cover and human disturbance in the modern Scottish Highlands. It supports treating animals as responsive participants rather than fixed targets. It does not establish the fictional animals’ detection cue, a universally safe distance, weapon efficacy, cooperative-hunting requirements or an ancient Iberian behaviour schedule. The solo logistical analysis and Day 48 choices remain authored physical reasoning.

Next evidence work

  • Specific local palaeoecology and species availability, including hazel productivity.
  • Experiments for bark containers, fire-kit woods, fibre yields, bone needles and thin rawhide loads.
  • Clay-body and firing trials with known temperatures and measured porosity.
  • Spindle throughput measured over harvesting, preparation, spinning and plying.
  • Projectile construction, shaft moisture, flexural behaviour and practice requirements.
  • Practical sanitation and first aid under the setting’s material constraints.

Record new evidence before marking a consequential procedure publication-ready.
